# Chapter 8: The One-Page Strategic Plan — The Tool for Strategic Planning

## Core Idea
The One-Page Strategic Plan (OPSP) compresses the company's vision into one action-oriented page so everyone aligns on one set of words, answering who, what, when, where, how, why, and "should we or shouldn't we?" Its left columns "preserve the core"; its right columns "stimulate progress."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **One-Page Strategic Plan (OPSP)**: Seven columns plus header, People/Process strip, and a bottom Strengths/Weaknesses/Trends band.
  - When to use: To align a scaling firm (or a division, one OPSP per unit as at JSJ), to make decisions at forks ("are we on strategy?"), and to run quarterly and annual planning.
  - How: Download the template; fill what you know first, like a crossword (Purpose and Brand Promise triangulate to the BHAG); "Get it down; then get it right." Start with Organization Name (short, what people actually say: FedEx, 3M, Big Ass Fans), your name, and date in DD Mon YYYY format.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Limited space forces prioritization, and it supplies a shared language plus a routine to keep the vision current. Failure: using it for marketing taglines (it's internal), filling it with random priorities not tied to the Critical Number, or never revisiting it.
- **OPSP columns**:
  - Col 1 (Should/Shouldn't): 3-8 Core Values/Beliefs.
  - Col 2 (Why): Purpose, Profit per X, BHAG, plus an "Actions" box: quarterly steps to keep Values/Purpose alive (e.g., a foosball table after an IPO for "serious fun").
  - Col 3 (Where, 3-5 years): Future date; Revenue (about 2x today); Profit (about 3x industry average); Market cap/cash/market share; Sandbox summary; Brand Promises with Kept Promise Indicators; 3-5 Key Thrusts/Capabilities (acquisitions, new lines, refocus like Jobs's four-product Apple); recruit an advisory board per Thrust.
  - Col 4 (What, 12 months): Financial outcomes, one annual Critical Number, a handful of Key Initiatives ("less is more").
  - Col 5 (How, 90 days): Quarterly financials, quarterly Critical Number (a step toward the annual one), 3-5 Rocks with a named owner each.
  - Col 6 (Finish Lines and Fun): Deadline, target, Theme name, Scoreboard design, Celebration, Reward.
  - Col 7 (Who): Individual or team KPIs, quarterly priorities, Critical Number, giving "line of sight."
  - How to choose the time frame: Ask "When will we double revenue?" (15%/yr = 5 years; 25% = 3 years); at 100% growth use 1 year / quarter / month ("dog years").
- **Critical Number**: The single "main thing that will be the main thing" for the year and quarter.
  - How: Choose one from the People/Balance Sheet side or the Process/P&L side, and pair it with a counterbalancing metric from the other side. Targets: Super Green / Green / Red (gold/silver/bronze).
- **People and Process strip (Reputation and Productivity)**: Balance six plates: employees, customers, shareholders (Reputation) and Make/Buy, Sell, Recordkeeping (Productivity). Track one or two weekly KPIs each (engagement scores; KPIs and NPS; cash and valuation; Lean speed/cost/quality; close ratio/sales cycle; data accuracy). It extends Kaplan and Norton's Balanced Scorecard from four to six.
- **Vision Summary**: Simplified one-pager for firms with 50 employees or fewer, or for communicating externally/onboarding: Core Values, Purpose, Brand Promises, BHAG, 3-5 year Key Thrusts, annual #1 Priority and initiatives, quarterly #1 Priority and Rocks, plus personal KPIs and Critical Number.
- **SWT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Trends)**: Senior-team replacement for SWOT.
  - When to use: Before a planning session, for strategic thinking by senior leaders.
  - How: List top 3 inherent strengths (core competencies) and weaknesses (play the hand you're dealt) plus 4-6 global trends from outside your industry and geography. Strengths/weaknesses anchor "preserve the core" (left); trends anchor "stimulate progress" (right).
  - Why it works / failure mode: SWOT causes inside/industry myopia, pulling executives into operational laundry lists and blinding them to disruptors (innovator's dilemma). Use SWOT only for middle managers.
- **Start/Stop/Keep survey**: Ask employees and customers what to start, stop, and keep doing.
- **Planning-session prep (4 activities)**: Gather feedback; middle management SWOT plus Top 3 priorities; senior SWT plus Top 3; keep learning.

## Key Concepts
- **Preserve the core / stimulate progress**: Collins's dual dynamic built into the OPSP layout.
- **Key Thrusts/Capabilities**: 3-5 major medium-term priorities (Col 3).
- **Key Initiatives**: The year's few "resolutions" aimed at the Critical Number.
- **Rocks**: 3-5 quarterly priorities (13-week sprints), each with an owner; from Covey's big rocks.
- **Critical Number**: The one measure that matters most per period.
- **Quarterly Theme**: Fun, memorable framing of the quarter's Critical Number.
- **Line of sight**: Visible link from daily work to company goals.
- **Brand Promise KPIs**: Measurable promise indicators (Rackspace's three rings, FedEx's 10 a.m.).
- **First/Second Law of Business Dynamics**: "Growth sucks cash!" and "Buy low, sell high!"

## Mental Models
- **Crossword puzzle**: Use when stuck; fill what you know and let it constrain the rest.
- **Camps on the way to Everest**: Use when setting 3-5 year targets (double revenue) toward the BHAG.
- **Big rocks first**: Use when the quarter is crowded; do the few big priorities before the pebbles.
- **Juggling six plates**: Use when improving one stakeholder or process might damage another.

## Anti-patterns
- **SWOT for the senior team**: Traps executives in operations and misses external trends.
- **Writing marketing copy in the OPSP**: It's an internal technical tool.
- **Too many priorities**: "Less is more"; random Initiatives dilute the Critical Number.
- **Ignoring the Actions box**: Values, Purpose, and BHAG get written and forgotten.
- **Hard-to-say names/ambiguous dates**: Alignment starts with names and formats.
- **Perfecting before shipping**: A good plan now beats a great plan late.

## Worked Example
JSJ Corporation (2,700 employees, six durable-goods firms) adopted the OPSP in 2006. Each business drafts its own OPSP by the October annual planning session; the plans are finalized over the following months, feed the JSJ parent OPSP, and are completed at a February board meeting. Surveys of customers (for SWOT) and employees (for talent) feed prep, and the senior team arrives a day and a half early at summits to update the plan. The OPSP helped decide to sell a California lithographic printing firm that "didn't fit," recycling proceeds into the remaining businesses; the result was continuously improving profit and growth.

## Key Takeaways
1. One shared page with common definitions beats a dozen conflicting documents.
2. The left three columns hold steady (core); the right columns flex each year and quarter.
3. Pick the planning horizon by asking when you will double.
4. One Critical Number per period, balanced against a counter-metric, plus 3-5 Rocks with owners.
5. Add a theme, scoreboard, celebration, and individual line of sight for execution.
6. Senior team uses SWT, middle managers use SWOT, and everyone answers Start/Stop/Keep.
7. Use the Vision Summary for small firms and communication.
