# Chapter 3: The Leaders — The FACe and PACe of the Company

## Core Idea
Most company problems trace back to unclear accountability at the top ("the bottleneck is always at the top of the bottle"). Use three one-page People tools (OPPP, FACe, PACe) to get leaders' personal goals, functional accountabilities, and process accountabilities explicit, with exactly one name per box.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **People Key Questions (the two tests)**: (1) Are you happy? (2) Would you enthusiastically rehire everyone (employees, customers, suppliers, investors) knowing what you know today?
  - When to use: before working on Strategy/Execution/Cash; unresolved relationship drains drain the energy for them. This is why People comes first in the 4 Decisions.
  - How: list the stakeholders causing friction (an executive, a disruptive employee, an oversized customer, a weak supplier); decide which to change, and have the crucial conversation.
- **One-Page Personal Plan (OPPP)**: personal parallel to the 4 Decisions, four columns: Relationships, Achievements, Rituals, Wealth, with the 5Fs (Faith, Family, Friends, Fitness, Finance) as a priority reminder.
  - When to use: each leader completes one so personal and professional goals are aligned.
  - How: (1) Relationships: list groups for a 10-25 year relationship, communities, 12-month and 90-day focus relationships, and relationships to end gracefully. (2) Achievements: short/medium/long-term, tied to those people. (3) Rituals: recurring routines (date night, peer coach, forum) and bad habits to stop. (4) Wealth: retirement, giving, experiences, assets vs. cash-draining liabilities. Treat wealth as a resource that flows through you.
- **Function Accountability Chart (FACe)**: one-page chart of the functions every company needs (titles deliberately omitted), with Person Accountable, 1-3 KPIs, and Results/Outcomes per function and business unit.
  - When to use: to diagnose people and performance gaps at the top; to decide which function to delegate next.
  - How: (1) Each exec writes initials of who is accountable per function/business unit; compare, since there is often no agreement. (2) Ask the four questions: more than one name in a box? One name in far more boxes than others? Any empty box? Enthusiastic about each person? (3) KPIs: 1-3 leading indicators per function, zero-based (cover the names first, then check if the person can deliver them). (4) Outcomes: take the P&L and balance sheet and assign one accountable person per line item.
  - Why it works / failure mode: a single point of accountability removes "Everybody/Somebody/Anybody/Nobody" gaps. Failure mode: CEOs avoid the changes because the people are friends, so the chart gets filled with "all of us" or overloaded names.
- **Process Accountability Chart (PACe)**: one-page chart of 4-9 key processes that drive the business, one accountable person and 1-3 KPIs (time, cost, quality) each.
  - When to use: when cross-functional processes (sales to cash, hiring, product launch) cause drama or delays.
  - How: (1) In two ~90-minute sessions name the 4-9 processes. (2) Assign one accountable person each; they report to the head of operations and monitor and lead a fix meeting, they are not the boss of everyone touching it. (3) Pick KPIs, with time as the key one. (4) Map with colored Post-its per function (include customers), cut waste, set KPIs at decision points. (5) Re-examine one process every 90 days in quarterly planning.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Lean removes what customers would not pay for. Failure mode: using Lean to cut headcount; people then sabotage it (Nurse Next Door never fires because of Lean).
- **Accountability vs. Responsibility vs. Authority**: Accountability = ONE person who counts/tracks and raises the flag; Responsibility = everyone who touches the issue; Authority = who makes the final call.
  - When to use: whenever a box, line item, or process has no clear owner.
  - How: assign exactly one accountable person; separately name who has authority (e.g., CFO accountable for cash, CEO authority, everyone responsible).
- **Lean and checklists**: process improvement approach plus checklists for key steps (Gawande's surgical checklist, Apple's APPLE service steps).
  - When to use: after processes are mapped, to make them run daily.

## Key Concepts
- **Cell division principle**: divide large teams into smaller units close to customers; no team too big to feed with two pizzas.
- **Matrix organization**: beyond ~50 employees, teams form around projects, products, segments, regions on top of functions.
- **Quasi-business units**: discrete teams by customer group, product line, or location, even without formal units.
- **Leading indicator**: a measurable activity that drives results, versus an outcome like profit.
- **Head of Company KPI**: the ratio of FACe boxes that have the right people doing the right things right.
- **Empty box**: a function nobody owns ("all of us" means none of us), e.g., Dell's vacant CMO and customer advocacy roles.
- **Right butts in right seats**: leaders must pass two tests: they don't need to be managed, and they regularly wow the team.
- **Zero-basing KPIs**: choose KPIs for the function from the business model, not from what the current person does.
- **Rotated P&L**: P&L turned 90 degrees so each exec owns a major line item.
- **Strength becomes weakness**: a founder's strength often becomes the company's weak spot because they hold on or hire too junior.

## Mental Models
- Use the "cell" model when customers or employees are confused about who to call: split teams and put each near its customers.
- Use "one name per box" when anything falls through the cracks: if more than one is accountable, no one is.
- Use "titles vs. functions" when founders are stuck as head of company: separate the job to be done from the title, so a founder can move to R&D, marketing, or customer advocacy.
- Use "organization as amplifier" when two departments fight: the conflict likely mirrors one between their leaders at the top.

## Anti-patterns
- **Shared accountability** (partners all listed as head of company): confusion and gaps.
- **One exec's name in 3-4+ boxes**: that person burns out or some functions are under-supported.
- **Hiring outside executives too fast**: bring in an outsider only every 6-9 months (time to find, embed culture, and repay salary), except for venture-backed or 100%-growth firms.
- **Hiring someone junior to "watch the details" of your strength**: stalls the company; hire someone better than you.
- **Letting functional heads boss business units in a matrix**: they must coach; raises and promotions should be informed by business unit heads.
- **Using Lean to cut people**: kills the initiative.
- **Detailed job descriptions**: replaced by Job Scorecards (Ch 4).

## Worked Example
Perly Fullerton (Ontario consultancy): six people in the room but only three names in the FACe boxes. Hires had been added, but the founders never gave up their senior responsibilities. After the long reallocation, Perly became president (vision, strategy) and Fullerton chief operating officer (drives execution). Dell: Michael Dell returned in 2007, found the CMO seat empty for two years and nobody leading customer advocacy among 20+ executives; he hired a CMO and moved a manufacturing leader to customer service, starting the turnaround.

## Key Takeaways
1. Run the two People tests (happy? rehire?) first; unresolved relationships drain energy for everything else.
2. One accountable person per function, process, and P&L/balance sheet line item; many names or none is a red flag.
3. Accountability is not authority; keep the three terms distinct.
4. Choose KPIs as leading indicators, zero-based from the business model, then check the person fits.
5. Define 4-9 processes with owners, map and Lean them, revisit one every 90 days, and add checklists.
6. Delegate FACe functions to leaders who need no management and wow the team; add outsiders slowly.
7. In a matrix, functional heads coach and business unit heads act like CEOs.
