# Chapter 13: The Accounting — Driving Profitability

## Core Idea
Accounting is the #2 weakness of growth firms after marketing. Co-written with Greg Crabtree (Simple Numbers), the chapter shows how to clear profit distortions, make gross margin dollars the top line, set real profit targets, and use labor efficiency to grow profitably and generate cash.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Simple Numbers (4 keys)**: (1) Clear the distortions, (2) Set appropriate profit targets, (3) Use labor efficiency to drive profitability, (4) Understand the 4 Forces of Cash Flow.
  - When to use: any firm under about $20M in revenue, or whenever reported profit and bank balance disagree.
  - How: work through the keys in order; do not set targets before the distortions are cleared.
- **Clear the Distortions**: (a) restate owner pay to a market wage; (b) replace revenue with Gross Margin Dollars as the internal top line.
  - When to use: before judging profit; owner distributions under $20M revenue hide true results.
  - How: reclassify owner distributions as a market-rate salary; define Gross Margin as revenue minus all NONLABOR direct costs (materials, subcontractors, pass-throughs); talk revenue to outsiders, gross margin internally.
  - Why it works / failure mode: it shows the real economic engine and ends revenue vanity. Failure mode: paying sales commissions on revenue when COGS varies, which lets salespeople set price; commission gross margin dollars instead.
- **Profit targets**: 5% pretax is life support, 10% doing well, 15% great, above 15% harvest it while you can (competition will arrive).
  - When to use: after distortions are cleared. How: if gross margin is under 40%, measure profit as a percent of gross margin, not revenue, to compare across industries.
- **Labor Efficiency Ratio (LER)**: the productivity multiplier of each labor dollar, in three segments. Direct LER = gross margin dollars / direct labor cost. Sales LER = contribution margin / sales labor cost. Management LER = contribution margin / management labor cost.
  - When to use: the #1 driver of profitability; use it to decide when to hire and how much.
  - How: contribution margin = gross margin minus direct labor; direct labor = people spending 50%+ of time delivering the product or service; everyone else is management; break out sales separately; never split a person across groups; count wages and bonuses only (no payroll taxes or benefits).
  - Why it works / failure mode: it turns labor from a cost percentage into a leverage multiple, so the only bottleneck is lacking the right people. Failure mode: hiring ahead of sales or filling every role at once.
- **Grow cycle ("10% is the new breakeven")**: hold labor cost flat until profit hits 15%, add labor (people, raises, bonuses) but never drop below 10%, then hold again until 15%; repeat.
- **4 Forces of Cash Flow**: Force 1 Taxes (set aside quarterly), Force 2 Manage Debt (clear the credit line), Force 3 Core Capital Target (two months of operating expenses in cash, excluding COGS), Force 4 Harvest Profits (dividends or bonuses).
  - When to use: in this order whenever cash is tight and the firm is not literally broke. How: fix profitability first.
- **Two Sets of Books**: external books for FASB and tax; internal books built on the rules you want to manage by (Dell expensed technology within 12 months).
- **Waterfall graphs**: rank gross margin or profit by customer, location, product line, salesperson or SKU.

## Key Concepts
- **Gross Margin Dollars**: the true top line; focus on dollars, not percent.
- **Contribution Margin**: gross margin minus direct labor (what accountants call gross profit, but it hides labor productivity).
- **Cost-led pricing**: in brutal markets the market sets price and you fit costs to it, maximizing gross margin dollars.
- **Core Capital Target**: two months of operating expenses in cash, after taxes, with nothing drawn on the line.
- **Trend analysis**: weekly visual charts; data over a week old is history.
- **Loss leader**: keep minimal and book as marketing expense.
- **Return on equity**: a 10% profit firm at its core capital target earns a minimum of 50% a year on equity.

## Mental Models
- Use gross margin dollars, not revenue, when comparing a distributor, builder, or staffing firm to a service firm.
- Use "10% is the new breakeven" when deciding whether to hire: hire only while profit stays at or above 10%.
- Use waterfall graphs when management time goes to problems: the unprofitable tail usually eats the attention.
- Use LER ranges (the level seen at each 15% profit) as the signal to add people or incentives, not gut feel.

## Anti-patterns
- **Keeping losers "for strategic reasons"**: Apple, Continental, Quiznos cut them; losing money for years is not strategy.
- **Commissions on revenue**: pays for volume, not margin.
- **Using the line of credit to patch cash**: it avoids the hard profitability decisions; draw only when profitable.
- **Buying assets to save tax**: spending $1 to save 40 cents; true wealth is after-tax profit.
- **Harvesting profits before debt is cleared and capital targets are met**: leaves the firm undercapitalized.
- **Growth regardless of profit**: reckless unless venture-backed.
- **Monthly or quarterly-only data**: hides early warnings (an electrical-supply distributor spotted a major customer's shrinking orders only via weekly charts).

## Worked Example
Two 50/50 owners boasted 25% profit; after market wages for the owners, true profit was 3%. The next year they earned market wages AND 20% profit. In the LER case ($450,000 monthly revenue, 4.4% profit): costs were held flat until revenue reached $492,195 per month (about 10%), with weak performers replaced. At 15% profit, Direct LER was about 4.25 each time; Sales LER was 8.29 the first time and 7.38 the second, so leaders waited until it returned to 8.29 before adding salespeople or incentives. Cash path: taxes reserved, line of credit retired, then three months of operating expenses in cash within 18 months. The $5M service firm in the intro grew to $25M at 10% profit after owner pay.

## Key Takeaways
1. Restate owner pay at market wage before reading profit.
2. Make gross margin dollars the internal top line.
3. Target 15% pretax; treat 10% as breakeven.
4. Manage labor by LER by segment, adding labor only when ratios say so.
5. Fix profitability before cash tricks; then taxes, debt, core capital, harvest.
6. Run internal books to your decision rules and review data weekly.
7. Add one senior hire at a time.
