# Chapter 14: The Power of One — 7 Key Financial Levers

## Core Idea
A healthy-looking P&L can hide a cash disaster. By viewing the business through the banker's lens (cash, returns, 4 drivers) and tweaking 7 financial levers by 1% or one day, leaders can scale on internally generated cash. Co-authored with Alan Miltz's Cash Flow Story team.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Power of One**: the cash and EBIT benefit of a 1% change (price, volume, COGS, operating expenses) or a one-day change (AR, inventory, AP) in each of the 7 levers.
  - When to use: to rank which levers deliver the most cash and to set achievable combined targets.
  - How: (1) compute the effect of 1% or one day on each lever (cashflowstory.com does it); (2) choose realistic combinations and increments; (3) convert them to KPIs with green, yellow, red ranges (good, average, bad); (4) review monthly by color; (5) assign each lever to an owning team.
  - Why it works / failure mode: small increments feel achievable and expose the true size of working-capital levers. Failure mode: expecting every lever to improve at once, or tracking only P&L levers.
- **The 7 Levers**: Price, Volume, Cost of goods sold (COGS)/direct costs, Operating expenses, Accounts receivable, Inventory/work in progress, Accounts payable.
  - How: the first four move profit; the last three move working capital (measured in days).
- **Funding = Operations balance sheet**: Equity + Net debt = Net operating assets (Working capital + Other capital; working capital = AR + inventory - AP).
  - When to use: to see how much of the business the bank, not owners, funds.
- **Return on Net Assets and the DuPont Formula**: EBIT / net operating assets = return on net assets, split into EBIT/Revenue (P&L efficiency) times Revenue/net operating assets (asset turnover, a sales effectiveness signal).
  - How: target a minimum 30% return on net assets for midsize firms; grow EBIT faster than net operating assets (between line B and line C in the author's option chart).
  - Why it works / failure mode: it combines P&L and balance sheet in one measure of strategy. Failure mode: strong margin but poor turnover masks unproductive capital.
- **4 Drivers**: Profitability, Working Capital Management (working capital days = (AR + inventory - AP) / sales x 100), Noncurrent Asset Management, Cash Flow/Funding.
- **Marginal Cash Flow**: compare gross margin cents per revenue dollar with working-capital cents per revenue dollar; if working capital is higher, every sale loses cash.
- **Operating cash flow**: EBITDA adjusted for working-capital movements; the bank's debt-service measure.

## Key Concepts
- **Growing broke**: revenue and profit rise while cash falls.
- **Hospital Division**: the bank's Special Credit/asset-management group that handles high-risk clients and makes quick exit decisions.
- **"Business speaks Spanish, banks speak Portuguese"**: operators and lenders use different financial languages.
- **Two uses of cash**: invest in growth, or fund management-influenced waste.
- **Cash flow (banker's definition)**: the change in cash and debt balances over a period; only cash and debt are facts, profit is an opinion.
- **Asset turnover**: revenue per $1 of net operating assets (2 to 3 expected in Gary's industry).
- **Equity is the most expensive funding**; debt is usually cheaper (within Crabtree's limits).
- **Cash-flow culture**: every team owns a lever with a visible KPI.

## Mental Models
- Use "Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, cash flow is king" when someone celebrates revenue or profit alone.
- Use the banker's view (what would the Hospital Division see?) when assessing your own financials or preparing for an investor or buyer.
- Use Power of One sensitivity when you need to pick targets: start with the lever that moves the most cash per unit of effort, usually AR or inventory days.
- Use marginal cash flow when volume grows: if working capital % exceeds gross margin %, growth consumes cash.

## Anti-patterns
- **Dismissing the bank as idiots**: they read cash capacity and your team's ignorance of it.
- **Satisfaction scored from the P&L only**: Gary rated himself 10 of 10 while cash fell.
- **Funding waste with debt**: new borrowing that covers poor management practice.
- **Tracking only profit levers**: working-capital levers hold most of the cash.
- **Ignoring the balance sheet**: the return and turnover insight lives there.
- **Trying to move all 7 levers at once**: pick achievable combinations.

## Worked Example
Gary's Furniture (revenue $42M, up from $35M; profit $2.1M up from $1.7M; EBITDA $5.6M). Cash flow 2013: short-term debt $5M to $7.3M plus long-term debt $9M to $10M = -$3.3M. Funding $26.9M (AR $8.6M, inventory $14.3M, fixed assets $9.5M, less AP $5.6M); bank put in about $2 per $1 of equity. Return on capital was 17.19% versus the 30% minimum; asset turnover $1.56 versus $2 to $3. Gross margin was 31 cents per $1 of revenue while working capital needed 41 cents ($17.4M / $42M), so each $1 of sales lost 10 cents (the bank funded $700K on the $7M growth). Operating cash flow was $1.2M against $1.2M tax, $1.4M interest, and $1M capex; cash flow before depreciation was -$4.3M despite EBITDA of $5.6M. A 1% or one-day gain on every lever would add over $1M cash and $900K EBIT. The chosen plan: cut AR by 5 days and inventory by 15 days, freeing over $1.7M and trimming the cash outflow from $3.3M to just under $1M. Roles: sales on price and gross margin, buyers on margin, finance and HR on operating expenses, sales and finance on AR, warehouse on stock, finance on AP; color-coded monthly KPIs.

## Key Takeaways
1. Cash and debt balances are the only facts; know your cash flow in dollars.
2. Cash is used to invest in growth or to fund waste; separate them.
3. Return on net assets = profit efficiency times asset turnover; aim for 30%+.
4. Check marginal cash flow before pushing volume.
5. Quantify each lever with the Power of One and set realistic combined targets.
6. Turn targets into green, yellow, red KPIs owned by specific teams.
7. Working-capital days usually offer the fastest cash.
