# Chapter 12: The Cash — Accelerating Cash Flow

## Core Idea
You can get by with decent People, Strategy, and Execution, but not a day without Cash, and "growth sucks cash." Shorten the Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC) deliberately, one initiative per 90 days, so growth is funded by internally generated cash instead of banks or investors.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)**: the number of days between spending a dollar on anything (rent, payroll, marketing, inventory) and having it return to the bank as cash. It can go negative (cash arrives before it is spent).
  - When to use: as the single KPI for how fast cash moves through the business; recalculate each quarter.
  - How: (1) read Churchill & Mullins, "How Fast Can Your Company Afford to Grow?" for the formulas; (2) calculate CCC in days; (3) calculate the cash needed to fund each extra day of CCC; (4) attack the days.
- **Cash Acceleration Strategies (CASh)**: the one-page brainstorming tool that splits the CCC into four cycles (selling, billing/payment, production/delivery, inventory/work in progress) and asks for ideas in each.
  - When to use: monthly management meeting (an hour or more, ideally with middle managers) and every 90-day planning session.
  - How: for each cycle, list ideas under the three categories below; also list moves on the 7 financial levers (Ch 14); pick ONE cash initiative as a quarterly priority (Rock).
  - Why it works / failure mode: it spreads cash awareness beyond finance, so every function sees how its decisions move cash. Failure mode: treating "this is just the way it is in our industry" as an answer, or running it once instead of every quarter.
- **Three improvement categories (shorten cycle times, eliminate mistakes, change the business model)**: every cash idea falls in one of these.
  - When to use: as the brainstorming grid inside CASh.
  - How: Shorten = Lean, faster sales cycles, faster invoicing and collection. Eliminate = fix invoice errors, incomplete orders, missed deadlines (the #1 cause of slow payment). Change model = deposits or prepayment, customer-funded growth (Costco membership fees), supplier-funded inventory (Dell).
- **Daily cash report**: the CFO reports cash in and out over the last 24 hours, why it changed, and the forecast for the coming month; charted weekly against AR and AP.
  - When to use: always; it lets you react in days, not months.

## Key Concepts
- **Growing broke**: a fast-growing firm that consumes more cash than it generates (Dell hit this in the mid-1990s with a 63-day CCC).
- **Negative CCC**: collecting a dollar before it must be spent; Dell reached -21 days.
- **Float**: pay your own expenses by credit card, and let customers pay by card so they can pay you fast.
- **Customer-funded business**: customers' cash (fees, deposits, prepayment) finances growth.
- **Core cash reserve**: great-by-choice firms hold 3 to 10 times more cash than rivals; Gates kept a year of operating expenses.
- **Recurring charges**: forever-expenses that quietly drain cash; the CEO personally approves any new one.
- **Profit improves cash**: better margins raise cash unless the balance sheet funds management waste.
- **Due date vs. terms**: print a fixed date ("May 31") instead of "net 30" so payables clerks pay on sign-off.

## Mental Models
- Use "one cash initiative per quarter" when cash feels like a vague worry: a single Rock beats ten scattered ideas.
- Use the CCC as a days-to-dollars converter when deciding if a change is worth it: every day cut frees roughly revenue divided by 365 in cash.
- Use "just ask" when a big payer is slow: large companies and governments often pay faster or prepay if asked repeatedly.
- Use the three categories (faster, fewer mistakes, new model) when brainstorming stalls in one cycle.

## Anti-patterns
- **"That's how our industry works"**: it blocks the questions that create Dell-style gains.
- **Net-30 invoices with no date**: the clock starts at an unknown sign-off.
- **Billing monthly while paying payroll twice a month**: a structural cash gap (Catapult Systems fixed it by billing twice a month; 90%+ of clients agreed).
- **Understaffed accounting**: late invoices and no collection follow-up; hire one person for invoicing and collections.
- **Ignoring why customers pay late**: the cause is often a mistake, a dissatisfied client, or an invoice format that does not fit their system.
- **Taking your eye off cash after a fix**: PPR relaxed in 2009 and had to redesign terms (advance payment).

## Worked Example
A $30M-revenue firm cuts its CCC by 30 days. Cash freed is about $30M x 30/365, roughly $2.5M. Options for it: pay down the credit line, pay a dividend, fund a growth project, hold for the right opportunity, or keep as insurance. Levers used by real firms: Catapult (twice-monthly billing, a charming collector who calls five days before due and the day after late); Salisbury Landscaping (deposits up front, one job at a time, walk-through "adjustment list" with the same crew, full payment at completion); PPR Talent (invoices customized per hospital billing code freed $1M a month, then advance payment); Benetton India (online reverse auction, bag price 52 to 34 cents, $1.2M saved).

## Key Takeaways
1. Cash, not profit, is the binding constraint when scaling.
2. Calculate CCC in days and know what one day of CCC costs.
3. Run CASh monthly and make one cash initiative a Rock every quarter.
4. Mistakes are the top cause of slow payment; speed and accuracy go together.
5. The biggest gains come from changing the model so customers or suppliers fund you.
6. Get a daily cash report from the CFO.
7. Review recurring charges every six months.
